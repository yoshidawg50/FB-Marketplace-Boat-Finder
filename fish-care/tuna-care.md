# Tuna Care

The tuna care chain, from the gaff shot that boats the fish to the slurry that
holds its quality for the drive home. Everything here is a quality problem: a
tuna that is bled, field-dressed, and chilled fast eats far better than one left
warm in the sun. The chain applies to [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md)
and other tuna; the steps below are parameters and judgment — for the hands-on
motions watch the linked videos rather than reading a play-by-play here.

## 1. Boat the fish cleanly (the gaff shot)

Getting the fish aboard without tearing it up is the first care step. See the
walkthrough for gaffing a bluefin from a boat:
[how to gaff a bluefin from a boat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w6DDCSLu8vM).

- Aim the gaff at the **head/shoulder** region, not the mid-body or tail — a
  head-forward shot controls the fish and keeps the gaff out of the meat.
- **Control the fish** before and during the shot: gaff on a committed pass with
  the fish's head coming toward you, not on a wild swing, so the gaff sets where
  you intend and the fish comes over the rail under control.

## 2. Bleed and gill-and-gut on the water

Do this immediately, while the fish is fresh, before it goes on ice. See the
walkthrough for a bluefin:
[gill & gut a bluefin (care on the water)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JeexIvtUkZc).

- **Bleed** the fish so the meat doesn't hold blood.
- **Field-dress** it: remove the **guts and gills**. Pulling the gills and
  viscera removes the warm mass and the enzymes that spoil quality fastest, so
  the slurry can then cool the carcass instead of fighting a bellyful of heat.
- Paraphrase, judgment: the point is to strip out everything that carries heat
  and spoilage before chilling — the video shows exactly where to cut and pull.

## 3. Wet-towel cooldown before icing

- Before the fish goes into the slurry, give it a **wet-towel cooldown** — knock
  the surface heat off the dressed carcass first. Dropping a hot fish straight
  into ice is less effective than letting it shed the worst of its heat, then
  committing it to the cold.

## 4. The below-freezing slurry

A saltwater slurry gets colder than plain ice water, which is what lets a big
tuna chill through to the core. (Slurry numbers from BD Outdoors care/seminar
reporting.)

- Mix **rock salt (~2 cups per 7–10 lb bag of ice)** with the ice, plus a
  **splash of seawater** to make a wet slurry. The salt drives the mix
  **below freezing**, which is the whole reason it out-chills bare ice.
- Keep the fish **fully in the slurry** and re-ice as the ice burns down —
  a fish only half in the cold chills unevenly.

## 5. Chill times by fish size

How long the fish needs in the slurry to be chilled through scales with its
mass. Hold it in the cold at least this long before you consider it done:

| Fish size | Chill time |
|---|---|
| 30–40 lb | ≈ 2 h |
| ~100 lb | ≈ 3–5 h |
| >100 lb | overnight, re-iced |

- A **>100 lb** fish will not core-chill in an afternoon — plan to hold it
  **overnight** and **re-ice** it as the slurry melts down.
